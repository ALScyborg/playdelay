Game night

How to sync a team’s radio broadcast with the TV picture

The local radio call and the television picture of the same college football or men’s basketball game are not the same feed, and they almost never arrive together. PlayDelay plays the team’s flagship radio stream in your browser and holds it until the whistle you hear is the whistle you see. Mute the TV, keep the picture, and dial the delay. This page is the long version of that idea: why the gap exists, what the player is actually doing, and how cable differs from a streaming app.

Why the TV is behind the radio

A flagship radio station is produced close to the stadium. The announce booth mixes the call, the station puts it on the air, and a stream of that same broadcast reaches PlayDelay a moment later. There is some delay in any stream, but it is small next to what happens to the video.

The picture you watch is a different production. Cameras go through the truck, the network encodes the show, and then your provider sends it to the house. Each of those steps waits for a buffer so a brief glitch does not freeze the screen. Cable and satellite add a few seconds of that processing. A live streaming app adds more, on purpose: YouTube TV, Hulu + Live TV, and similar apps hold a chunk of video so the picture can keep playing when your internet connection dips. That buffer is why the radio announcer often describes a run you have not seen yet.

Your own screen can add a little more. Motion smoothing on the television, a soundbar that lipsyncs, or an app playing on a phone instead of the cable box all change the gap. The large, annoying gap is still the video path. The radio crew is not calling the play early. They are closer to live, and the picture is late.

PlayDelay cannot speed the television up. It can only hold the radio. That is the right direction for the usual problem — you hear the play, then you see it. If the picture is already ahead of the radio, adding delay makes the mismatch worse. The steps below start by checking which one is early.

What PlayDelay’s delay does

The player loads the flagship stream listed for that team on the team radio page. In browsers that allow it, the audio passes through a ring buffer that can hold up to 120 seconds. You choose how much of that buffer to use. The presets are Live, 5s, 8s, 10s, and 15s. The row under them moves the delay by 1, 5, or 10 seconds in either direction. On a computer, the up and down arrow keys nudge by one second. Space plays or pauses, and M mutes.

When you press Play with a delay already selected, the player does not let the live call leak out while it fills the buffer. It goes quiet, the status shows that it is loading, and a note says Building delay…. That quiet stretch lasts about as long as the delay you asked for. A 15-second setting means about 15 seconds of silence, then the call starts already late. When the buffer is full the player says Synced. The silence is the delay being built. The stream did not freeze.

After audio is playing, a change larger than about a second and a half takes effect immediately, so jumping from 8 seconds to 15 does not crawl there. You may hear the call skip. One-second nudges are the tool for the last bit of alignment, once you are close.

The delay you set can be remembered on your account, so the next game on the same television source starts near the right number. A different source needs its own number. The cable box in the den and the YouTube TV app on the same TV are not the same delay.

Some streams refuse the browser audio path the delay engine needs. When that happens the player says so and falls back to live playback with no delay. You still hear the station. You cannot dial a delay on that fallback. If the radio is ahead of the picture, the television or the cable box is the thing that has to wait. If the radio is behind the picture, you are already as close as PlayDelay can get on that stream: Live.

Step by step

  1. Put the game on the television and mute the TV speakers. Two calls at once makes it impossible to judge the gap, and it is the thing you were trying to avoid.
  2. Sign in. PlayDelay requires an account (email and a password of at least six characters). Favorites you save show up first in the player.
  3. Pick the team whose station you want. Football and men’s basketball schedules both come from ESPN. The radio stream is the same flagship for both sports when a stream exists. Kansas State’s schedule is there; its stream is not connected yet.
  4. Tap Play. If you already know this television runs behind, set 8 or 15 seconds before you press Play so the first audio you hear is already delayed. Otherwise start at Live and listen for one sharp event: a snap, a whistle, a kick, or the horn. Crowd noise is a poor cue. It is mixed differently on radio and on television.
  5. If you hear the event before you see it, add delay. If you see it before you hear it, lower the delay toward Live.
  6. Stop when the whistle and the picture hit together. Leave it. The next nudge is only worth making if the picture rebuffers and jumps.

Streaming apps versus cable

These are starting points, not measurements. PlayDelay does not detect whether you are on a cable box or an app. You match the screen you are watching.

  • Cable or satellite box. Start at 8 seconds. Nudge by one second around the next whistle. Five, ten, or fifteen seconds is a common neighborhood. You should not need to climb toward a minute.
  • Over-the-air antenna. An antenna picture is often closer to live than cable. Try 5 seconds, or Live, before you assume you need the longer presets.
  • YouTube TV, Hulu + Live TV, and other streaming apps. Expect a longer gap, because the app is buffering video so playback does not stutter. Start at 15 seconds. If the call is still early once the picture is stable, add 10 seconds at a time. Do not chase the first play while the app is still spinning up; wait until the picture is moving normally, then set the delay.
  • A phone, tablet, or laptop app (the network app, a stream in a browser) is its own source. It can be later than the cable box in the same room. Set the delay for the screen you are looking at.

Do not stack delays. If the television has an audio-delay or lipsync setting and PlayDelay is also holding the radio, you will overshoot and then “correct” in the wrong direction. Mute the TV audio and let PlayDelay be the only delay.

A streaming app can also rebuffer in the middle of a quarter. The picture jumps later, the radio is suddenly early again, and a few seconds of nudge fixes it. You do not need to reload the player unless it shows an error. Live streams in PlayDelay try to reconnect on a stall, up to five attempts inside a minute, and then they show the usual error banner.

When the match will not land

  • Building delay… sits there. It should last about as long as the delay you set, plus a short margin. A 30-second delay takes about 30 seconds to fill. If it never releases, tap Play again. If the player then says the delay engine is unavailable, you are on live audio only.
  • The radio is still early at 120 seconds. That video path is slower than PlayDelay can hold. Switch to a source with less buffer — a cable box or an antenna instead of a streaming app — or watch a feed that is not sitting on a long buffer. Adding more delay is not possible past two minutes.
  • You see the play first, and the delay is already Live. The radio is the late one. PlayDelay cannot pull the picture backward. Turn off any extra video processing you added, or switch to a slower picture if you need the radio to catch up. The player will also tell you this: lower the delay, or delay the TV when the radio is behind.
  • No sound. Unmute the player (the button reads Unmute when it is muted), raise the volume slider, and tap Play. The TV being muted is correct. The player being muted is not.
  • ASU is silent or errors on the road. Arizona State’s game-day stream can be geo-blocked outside Phoenix. The player still attempts the station’s continuous mount, which sometimes plays when the game-day feed does not.
  • Kansas State will not play. The schedule loads. The stream is not connected, and the player says so. Pick another team’s station if you want audio tonight.
  • The call drifts every few minutes. That is usually the video app rebuffering, not the radio speeding up. Nudge one second. If the player banner says the stream stalled and switched to live playback, your delay was cleared because the fallback cannot delay. Set it again after a successful Play, or delay the television.

A few habits that make the sync stick

  • Judge the gap on a snap, whistle, or horn. Crowd swell and the public-address announcer are mixed on different delays and will lie to you.
  • Keep the phone awake and the volume up on a speaker or headphones you can hear over a room. The player is built with large buttons so the delay can be changed with one hand.
  • Write down the number that worked for this television and this app. Next Saturday, start there. When you travel and watch in a bar, expect a different number.
  • Basketball uses the same stations as football. The delay follows the screen, not the sport. A conference tournament stream on a phone will not match the delay you used for a Saturday football broadcast on cable.
